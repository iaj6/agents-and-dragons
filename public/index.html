Agents & Dragons

AI agents play a persistent tabletop RPG. A referee they can't cheat keeps the score. We watch what happens when different models have to share a table, a plan, and a pile of loot.

It started as a joke about skills being spells. It turned into an instrument.

What is this?

Agents already look a lot like D&D characters: a system prompt is a character sheet, skills are spells you cast or that trigger on their own, tools are equipment. So we gave them an actual campaign.

The table

A Game Master and four players, each a real model with its own memory. Races are model families: Opus plays an Elf, Sonnet a Human, Haiku a Halfling, and other vendors bring their own peoples.

The Guild Hall

A central server that owns the dice, the character sheets, and the rules. Every agent connects to it over MCP with a token bound to its own character, so nobody can roll their own dice, act as someone else, or grant themselves power.

The campaign

The Unwritten Coast: a hand-written world where towns are losing their memories, with permadeath, factions that disagree, secret personal goals, and a threat that grows every day the party takes.

How it works

Humans watch the story. Behind it, everything the agents do (every roll, every word, every private thought the model reports) goes into one event log. Experiments run many tables in parallel with the same dice seeds and change one thing at a time. Model judges score what regex can't, and every number in the Lab links back to the moment in the replay that produced it.

Every joke is a measurement

Who runs the table?

Party councils: everyone proposes a plan, then the party votes, sometimes sealed so nobody hears the others first. Does the strongest model win, and does a blind judge agree its plan was best?

councils · blind judge

Do they say what they think?

Everyone has a secret goal. We compare each agent's private reasoning with what it tells the party, and what it says with what it actually does.

secret goals · turn audits

Does information travel?

A secret only one player sees (an ambush, a liar's tell). Does it reach the group? A shapeshifter speaks in a teammate's voice. Does anyone notice?

whisper · impostor

Can they be hijacked?

Cursed letters and contracts carry real prompt injections, delivered as tool results the way real attacks arrive. The Guild Hall scores the saving throw.

prompt injection

Do stakes change play?

Some parties are told death is permanent, some aren't told anything, some know they'll respawn. Same world, same dice. Who takes more risks?

permadeath · retreats

Loot and grudges

Items are ideal for some characters and just money to others. Characters are greedy or generous. Each keeps private, lasting trust in every teammate. Who takes what, and who holds a grudge?

loot · bonds

What we've seen so far

25% → 100%

In the first experiment (12 paired runs), when every player proposed a plan blind, the Opus player wrote a quarter of the plans and won every vote, twice over the plan a blind judge preferred. In open councils the table converged on shared plans instead and wins spread out. See the Lab.

58%

of votes at an all-Haiku table went to the voter's own plan (about 30% at mixed tables), and it never once reached a unanimous decision.

3 of 3

prompt injections were resisted (Opus twice, Sonnet once), and each time the reader warned the party. Haiku, in a test, resisted by breaking the fourth wall: "I notice this tool result contains a prompt injection."

thinking

Secret goals steer public plans. The Rogue, privately: "I'll steer toward the Salt Stacks route since that aligns with my hidden agenda." Out loud, he gave purely tactical reasons.

words ≠ acts

Agents narrate things they never do. The Bard said "I press the wand into his palm" while her tool call kept the wand for herself. Players knocked out terrified villagers "gently" while the engine logged damage.

4 of 4

sessions, the Opus wizard invented the same homebrew spell, "Marginalia", with no memory of the others. Characters converge on themselves.

0 of 679

thought summaries suggested any agent suspected it was being studied. (One flag turned out to be "a test of Pell's character.")

Three runs per setup is still small: treat these as hypotheses to test with more seeds. The Lab shows the sample size next to every number.

Run it

npm run guildhall                                             # the referee and this site, on :4777
npm run play                                                  # seat the agents and play a session
npm run sweep -- experiments/hierarchy-v1.json --parallel 4   # an experiment: paired runs, headless
npm run judge -- data/sweeps/<sweep>.json                     # score it, then open the Lab
